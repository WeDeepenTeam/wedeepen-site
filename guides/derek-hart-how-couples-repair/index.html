Derek Hart on How Couples Repair: Say What's Hurting

By WeDeepen · October 25, 2026

The short answer

On episode 29 of Christina Weber's podcast, couples coach Derek Hart explains that most struggling couples aren't incompatible; their communication stopped working years ago. Repeated fights are about the bond feeling at risk, not the topic. His approach: slow down, say the one sentence that's been held in, and let your partner take it in. Three to five of those moments, he says, can make a couple feel secure again.

Derek Hart is a couples coach who has worked with couples for more than three decades. He found the work after trying seven times to leave a relationship that wasn't good for him, and spending years in Co-Dependents Anonymous figuring out "how do people relate, how do they connect, what is really going on here." Christina Weber sat down with him in Miami after he gave the keynote at the first RelateFest. In episode 29 of her podcast, he explained how he helps couples on the edge of breaking up feel close again. This guide sums up that conversation. Quotes are from the episode transcript, lightly edited for readability.

Incompatible, or just stuck?

When couples arrive asking whether they should stay together, Derek's first question is whether they have a real incompatibility. One person wanting to sail the world and the other wanting a farm is one. But that's rare. "Couples usually don't actually have an incompatibility. What they have is communication that hasn't worked, often for years, sometimes for decades."

Sometimes, after real work, it turns out one partner doesn't want the depth the other wants. That is an incompatibility, and he treats it as one. His stated goal with every couple is honest about both outcomes: "to get closer as soon as possible, or to break up as soon as possible."

Most couples ask for help too late

Derek once tried to estimate how many couples ever see a couples counselor. Working from the number of licensed counselors in a city, how much of their practice is couples, and the local population, he landed at roughly one in 400. That's his own back-of-the-envelope figure, not a published study, but it matches what he sees: "They come very, very late in the game." By then the pain is with them all day, and it affects their kids, their friendships, and their work.

His advice runs the other way. "If you were brilliant enough to come to see a marriage counselor in the first three weeks of your relationship, because you both had things from the past that snuck up and tripped you up, that would be absolutely brilliant." Before that, he suggests reading good books on relationships, naming Sue Johnson's Hold Me Tight, and getting honest about your own pattern. In his experience, everyone does one of a few things: "you hide, you run away, or you blame, or you build up resentment."

The sentence that opens everything

Derek doesn't need a couple's history to start. He watches them talk about in-laws, money, the house, the kids, and looks for what he calls emotional signals. "Every couple that comes has a sentence that they could say to the other that would open up everything."

He gave an example. He slows the couple down, has them make eye contact, and helps one partner say the thing underneath the complaints, something like: "I remember how it used to be when we first connected, and I miss that so much. Where's the person that was my best friend?" Often she starts crying, because that's what has been held in.

Then her partner gets defensive, because all he hears is that he's doing it wrong again. So Derek helps him reveal what's under that: "I feel blamed. I feel like I can never get it right with you." Both people are hurting, both are bracing, and neither has said it. Within the first 15 or 20 minutes, couples often realize "this is what I've needed to say for five years."

You're never fighting about dinner

This may be the most useful idea in the episode. When a couple starts the same argument they've had a thousand times, "they are not talking about the topic they think they're talking about. They are talking about their bond being in peril, every single time." Even a fight over Chinese or Italian food, he says, is really: "I don't feel loved." "Well, I don't either."

That moment, the familiar argument starting up, is the signal to stop arguing the topic and switch to vulnerability and attunement. Couples who can't solve problems, he says, usually can't because the bond feels unsafe. "We need to make their bond secure so that they can solve their own problems."

Vulnerability, then attunement

Derek separates the two. Vulnerability is "dropping in to the thing that I am scared about" and saying it: I'm scared, I'm confused, when you leave I feel abandoned. Attunement is what happens next, often before a word is spoken back. "The eye contact between the two, before the other person even responds, that's emotional attunement." Does the listener see that this was hard to say, and that it matters?

His measure of progress is specific. When a couple has "somewhere between three and five actual bonding moments in front of me, where they feel deeply how they've affected the other person, and the other person receives that and feels gotten," and then another 10 to 12 on their own, he says they're securely reattached.

What men want most

Christina asked what he sees men wanting. His answer: the criticism usually starts early, men skip over how much it hurts, and resentment builds without either partner noticing. But underneath, "he just wants to figure out how to make her happy." Even after years of resentment, "if he does something right in the session with me and she likes it and smiles, he's in heaven immediately."

FAQ

Who is Derek Hart?

Derek Hart is a couples coach with more than 30 years of experience helping couples on the edge of breaking up. He gave the keynote at the first RelateFest in Miami.

How does Derek Hart tell if a couple is incompatible?

He asks whether the problem is a true incompatibility, like wanting completely different lives, or communication that hasn't worked for years. In his experience it's usually the second.

What are couples really fighting about?

According to Derek, a familiar argument is almost always about the bond feeling at risk, not the topic. That's the moment to slow down and share what's underneath.

How many sessions does it take?

He often talks about the first eight hours, either weekly sessions or an intensive, and describes a couple as reattached after three to five bonding moments in session and 10 to 12 at home.
